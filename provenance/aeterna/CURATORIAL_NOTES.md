# AETERNA — Curatorial Notes

## Reading the exhibition

AETERNA follows the changing meanings of Roman sculpture through portraiture, bodies, relief, colour, and survival. Its visual scenes are contemporary interpretations, supported by the museum research below. Dates for ancient works remain approximate where the collections describe them that way.

The exhibition’s ivory surfaces evoke how surviving sculpture is often encountered today. **Ancient stone was often painted.** The absence of visible colour in a modern display should not be taken as the complete ancient appearance.

## Likeness and verism

Roman veristic portraits could emphasize age, wrinkles, and bodily imperfections to suggest experience and civic virtue. These features communicated a chosen public identity. Realistic heads could also be joined to idealized heroic bodies.

Source: [The Met — Roman Portrait Sculpture: The Stylistic Cycle](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/roman-portrait-sculpture-the-stylistic-cycle).

## The imperial image

Imperial portrait types circulated through sculpture and coins, making a ruler recognizable across the empire. Idealization and family resemblance helped express continuity. Damaged or recarved portraits show that an image’s political meaning could change.

Sources: [The Met — Republican through Constantinian](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/roman-portrait-sculpture-republican-through-constantinian) and [The Stylistic Cycle](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/roman-portrait-sculpture-the-stylistic-cycle).

## A body in balance

Contrapposto places most of a standing figure’s weight on one leg. The hips, shoulders, and limbs adjust in response. Roman sculpture adapted Greek bodily ideals; the Herakles shown here reaches us through a later museum cast.

Sources: [Getty — Working with Sculpture](https://www.getty.edu/education/teachers/classroom_resources/curricula/sculpture/background1.html), [Getty — Explore Statue of Hercules](https://www.getty.edu/education/k-12-learning/explore-statue-of-hercules/), and [SMK — KAS224](https://open.smk.dk/en/artwork/image/KAS224).

## Relief and myth

The Roman wellhead combines the water-related stories of Narcissus and Echo, and Hylas with the nymphs. Carved figures turn a functional object into a setting for myth.

Source: [The Met — Roman wellhead, 2019.7](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/775805).

## Ancient colour

Roman marble could receive selective colour, extensive paint, gilding, or inlay. AETERNA’s ivory presentation evokes a modern encounter with surviving sculpture. The historical notes acknowledge fragmentary pigment evidence and the uncertainty of exact reconstruction.

Source: [The Met — Polychromy of Roman Marble Sculpture](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/polychromy-of-roman-marble-sculpture).

## Fragments and restoration

Surviving sculpture may combine ancient material with later repairs or additions. Historical restoration has sometimes changed the form we inherit. AETERNA’s disassembly is an artistic simulation; its fragment boundaries do not document ancient damage, an excavation sequence, or a conservation treatment.

Source: [Getty — Statue of an Emperor: A Conservation Partnership](https://www.getty.edu/art/exhibitions/statue_emperor.html).

## Scans, casts, and imagined images

The anatomical model derives from SMK’s public-domain plaster cast of the Lansdowne Herakles, acquired in 1897. The wellhead derives from a Met Open Access scan of the Roman object. Generated portrait images are imagined studies without an assigned historical identity. Altered lighting, materials, and motion belong to this digital interpretation.

Sources: [SMK object record](https://open.smk.dk/en/artwork/image/KAS224), [SMK official metadata](https://api.smk.dk/api/v1/art/?object_number=KAS224), [Met wellhead record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/775805), and [The Met’s 3D programme](https://www.metmuseum.org/press-releases/3-d-models-announcement-2026).

## Interpretation and attribution

- Generated portraits have no assigned historical identity. They do not establish the likeness of Augustus, Marcus Aurelius, or any named ancient individual.
- Lighting and material presentation are artistic treatments. Ancient colour is discussed in the notes; there is no pigment-reconstruction control.
- The 36-part Herakles animation is a contemporary visualization. Its simplified geometry, smoothed surfaces, and newly created interior cuts do not document historical breakage or a restoration sequence.
- The sculpture models, historical writing, and generated imagery have different origins. See [the asset overview](ASSET_PROVENANCE.md), [the museum model records](MUSEUM_ASSETS.md), and the in-exhibition research notes.
- Museum references and public-domain credits do not imply institutional endorsement of AETERNA.
