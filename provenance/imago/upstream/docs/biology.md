# IMAGO — biology, English copy, and image review

Research checked 9 November 2026. This is an editorial working document for the IMAGO build. All proposed website copy below is original English prose. No source photographs or institutional layouts are licensed or copied by this research.

## Editorial contract

The protagonist is one **imagined monarch butterfly, *Danaus plexippus***. Its setting, light, weather, camera, and visual continuity are artistic decisions, not a documented observation of a real individual. The order of life stages follows biological evidence. Scrolling changes what the visitor observes; it does not model elapsed time or accelerate a living animal.

Suggested global note: **“An illustrated natural history. These images are artistic reconstructions of one monarch’s life, informed by the sources below.”**

Suggested chronology note: **“A life unfolds over time. Scrolling lets us revisit its stages.”**

Required schematic label: **“Developmental schematic — not measured internal imagery.”**

## Ten chapters: reusable English copy

Each main paragraph is approximately 25–45 words. The second beat belongs in the interaction or detail area; it must remain available in reduced-motion mode.

### 01 — A name still folded

**Hero title:** IMAGO  
**Subtitle:** Wings within  
**Opening line:** Change sometimes begins in almost perfect stillness.

**Main paragraph:** A small green shape hangs beneath a branch. This is a chrysalis: one chapter in a monarch’s life. Before it came an egg and a caterpillar. After it, another body will meet the same world.

**Beat 1:** Recognize a pupa, with the whole outline and suspension visible.  
**Beat 2 / expanded stage introduction:** “Egg. Caterpillar. Chrysalis. Adult. Four distinct stages, one continuous life. We begin in the middle, then look back.”

**Controls:** “Where are we?” · “Look back to the beginning”  
**Source:** S2, S5. The particular branch and individual are narrative inventions.

### 02 — A small light beneath a leaf

**Main paragraph:** Earlier, the story rested beneath a milkweed leaf. A single pale egg, ribbed from tip to base, held the beginning. The leaf was more than a place to wait: it would become food for the caterpillar.

**Beat 1 / wide view:** “One egg beneath a milkweed leaf.”  
**Beat 2 / close view:** “Raised ribs run along the shell. This close view shows the same egg, in the same place.”

**Controls:** “Look closer” · “Show the whole leaf”  
**Source:** S3 for egg ridges; S5 for milkweed. Avoid a false measured scale.

### 03 — A leaf, changed

**Main paragraph:** The newly hatched caterpillar is pale, without the bold stripes we recognize. Later, in its third instar, those bands are clear. As it feeds along the leaf’s edge, its growing body leaves a small absence behind.

**Beat 1 label:** “Just hatched”  
**Beat 2 label:** “Later · third instar”  
**Detail:** “An instar is a stage between molts. These views show different moments.”

**Controls:** “Just hatched” · “Third instar”  
**Source:** S1. Do not label a striped third-instar animal as newly hatched. Older first-instar larvae can acquire stripes; the pale claim applies specifically to fresh hatchlings.

### 04 — Outgrowing the old shape

**Main paragraph:** Growth asks for more than a larger outline. The caterpillar sheds an outer skin that no longer fits, revealing a soft new surface. Across successive instars, its body changes while the life within it continues.

**Beat 1 / molt detail:** “A new surface, with the shed skin beside it.”  
**Beat 2 / instar strip:** “Five larval instars. Illustrative sizes, not a measuring scale.”

**Controls:** “Compare instars” · “Return to the molt”  
**Source:** S2 for molting; S5 for five instars.

### 05 — The weight of a thread

**Main paragraph:** Feeding gives way to a search for support. The caterpillar makes a small silk pad, then hangs in a J. Its final larval skin is shed, revealing the green pupa beneath. The attachment remains the center of everything.

**Beat 1 / J view:** “Hanging from its rear end, the caterpillar curls its head upward.”  
**Beat 2 / pupa view:** “A hooked structure called the cremaster secures the chrysalis to the silk pad.”

**Controls:** “Hanging in a J” · “Forming a chrysalis”  
**Source:** S4 for prepupal sequence; S3 for the cremaster. A real silk pad is not a long decorative thread or a woven cocoon.

### 06 — A quiet reconstruction

**Main paragraph:** The outside offers little to watch. Inside, development continues. Adult structures take shape as tissues change and reorganize. This is a connected process, already underway before the chrysalis formed—not an entire animal disappearing and starting again.

**Beat 1 / outside:** “An apparently quiet surface.”  
**Beat 2 / schematic:** “A simplified relationship between larval growth and adult development.”

**Controls:** “See the surface” · “See the schematic”  
**Required caption:** “Developmental schematic — not measured internal imagery.”  
**Source:** S2 for tissue reorganization; S1 for development already underway. Schematic regions must not imply measured positions of organs.

### 07 — Color arrives first

**Main paragraph:** Near the end of this stage, orange, black, and white become recognizable beneath the covering. Pigment develops in the wing scales. The familiar outline remains, while the pattern of the adult begins to announce itself.

**Beat 1:** “The first recognizable pattern.”  
**Beat 2 / observer:** “Compare separate stages of development. Your selection changes the view, not the animal’s age.”

**Controls:** “Early” · “Middle” · “Late” · “Side by side” · “Single view” · “Reset observation”  
**Initial state:** Middle.  
**Source:** S1. Do not describe the covering as turning into transparent glass.

### 08 — Leaving a former shape

**Main paragraph:** The adult emerges through an opening in the pupal covering. It finds a grip and hangs beneath the empty shell. Its wings are still soft and folded. Leaving the chrysalis is a beginning, not an immediate invitation to fly.

**Beat 1 / sequence:** “The opening. The emergence. The grip.”  
**Beat 2 / resting frame:** “Out of the chrysalis; still supported by it.”

**Controls:** “Previous view” · “Next view”  
**Suggested four-view labels:** “The covering opens” · “The body emerges” · “Finding a grip” · “Resting beneath the shell”  
**Source:** S4. Each frame must show one physically continuous adult and its relationship to the shell.

### 09 — Open, then ready

**Main paragraph:** The new wings unfold as the butterfly pumps hemolymph into them. They gain their shape, then need time to dry and stiffen. Forewings and hindwings become readable as distinct forms, carrying a surface of overlapping scales.

**Beat 1 / expansion:** “Body fluid helps the wings expand; this is not inflation with air.”  
**Beat 2 / structure detail:** “Two forewings and two hindwings meet at the thorax.”

**Controls:** “Forewing” · “Hindwing” · “Body” · “Whole butterfly”  
**Source:** S6 for hemolymph and scales; S4 for drying; S3 for wing pairs.

### 10 — Leave the sky to it

**Main paragraph:** At last, the branch no longer holds the whole story. The butterfly has gone; the empty chrysalis remains at its old attachment. Return to any stage and the shapes seem less separate: each belonged to this one imagined life.

**Closing line:** The shape left behind is a trace of having been here.

**Beat 1:** Departure followed by a complete, quiet environmental composition.  
**Beat 2:** A stage review that restores correct chapter states.

**Controls:** “Watch it leave” · “See the final view” · “Look again”  
**Source:** This paragraph is artistic narration, grounded in the preceding life history. It does not assert a recorded individual’s movements. Do not imply same-day reproduction.

## Image and sequence review criteria

These are production constraints informed by the sources, not claims that generated images have been scientifically measured.

### Global continuity

- One individual at a time, unless a clearly labeled comparison intentionally shows different moments.
- Preserve the same branch attachment, side light, camera direction, and protagonist silhouette in aligned chrysalis comparisons.
- Do not imply a scientific photograph, CT scan, measured organ map, or exact clock through styling or captions.
- Keep visible plant and body contacts credible. Nothing floats, intersects solid matter, or acquires duplicate heads or wing pairs.
- A source photograph is reference evidence, not an automatically redistributable asset.

### Egg and larva

- Egg: opaque pale shell with longitudinal ridges, not a crystal containing a tiny finished butterfly.
- Newly hatched larva: muted pale body; later striped scenes require an explicit time/stage label.
- Caterpillar: one head and continuous segments; thoracic legs and abdominal prolegs remain attached to the body and substrate. The long fleshy filaments are not the small true antennae (S3).
- A leaf notch persists while that feeding moment is revisited. Reverse scrolling revisits the narrative, rather than undoing biology or generating a new feeding event.

### Suspension and pupa

- J pose: rear attached above, head at the lower curved end. The silk pad is small and load-bearing, not a large hanging cord (S4).
- The pupa’s cremaster meets the silk pad; avoid a necklace loop or adhesive-free hovering (S3).
- Chrysalis is the pupa; cocoon would describe a silken covering. Do not cover this monarch’s pupa in woven silk (S2).
- The late stage retains a material pupal covering. Avoid crystal refraction or a fully spread adult squeezed inside a green ornament.

### Emergence and adult

- Separate frames show opening, bodily emergence, grip, and rest. Do not simulate different body topologies by alpha-crossfading two adults.
- Newly emerged wings remain wrinkled and compact; the abdomen may appear relatively large (S4).
- Four wings in total, with distinguishable forewing and hindwing geometry. A side-on view may hide the far pair (S3).
- Monarchs have six legs, but the front pair is reduced and held near the thorax; four conspicuous supporting legs can be correct. Never demand six equally long visible legs (S3).
- Wing enlargement is hemolymph-assisted expansion, not air inflation. Drying and readiness follow emergence (S6, S4).
- Keep one chosen adult sex/pattern consistent; if the rendering is not sufficient to identify sex reliably, omit a sex label rather than infer it.

## Research-to-implementation notes

1. A simplified stage diagram should show relationships and labels, not invent organs in precise anatomical locations.
2. Use a cropped detail from the same egg or wing image for magnification. A separately generated image needs correspondence review before it can claim to be that same detail.
3. “First instar” and “just hatched” are not interchangeable appearance labels: later first-instar individuals can show dark striping (S1).
4. The chapter-7 control is a comparison of discrete prepared views. No continuous biological-age slider is implied.
5. A displayed green-to-patterned sequence is an artistic reconstruction. Keep its shared silhouette for observation, but do not call image registration a measured specimen record.
6. Sources contain broader claims and figures that the site does not need. Avoid importing exact durations, reproductive numbers, migration distances, or husbandry instructions.
