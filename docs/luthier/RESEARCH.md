# LUTHIER — Research and Art Direction

Access date for every source below: **2026-10-07**.

This dossier separates verified construction and acoustics from original art direction. The experience represents an unnamed violin; it is not a reconstruction of a particular historical instrument.

## Factual foundation

| Subject | Concise finding | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spruce | A conventional top uses spruce, with fine longitudinal grain. | R2 |
| Maple | Maple commonly forms the back, ribs, neck, scroll and bridge. Its rippled figure comes from wavy wood fibers. | R2 |
| Ebony | Ebony commonly forms the fingerboard and other fittings; other dense woods are also used. | R2 |
| Documented finish | The Met's 1693 Gould violin has a two-piece spruce top, tightly figured two-piece maple back and orange-brown varnish. | R1 |
| Bridge | Strings alone move little air. The bridge and body transfer their vibration into audible radiation. The bridge is held by string pressure, rather than glue. | R3, R6 |
| Internal structure | The sound post fits between top and back near the E-string bridge foot. The bass bar lies beneath the top on the G-string side. | R12 |
| F-holes | F-holes connect internal and external air and affect flexibility of the central top. | R3 |
| Resonance and energy | The plates and enclosed air have resonances. The body changes how efficiently bow energy becomes sound; it does not add energy. | R3 |
| Bowing | Rosined bow hair repeatedly grips and releases the string. Many stick-slip cycles occur during one stroke. Continuous bowing supplies energy and sustains harmonic content. | R4 |
| Nodal patterns | Chladni lines mark regions of minimal motion. Loose material collects there after leaving more active regions. Free-plate patterns do not directly describe every response of an assembled instrument. | R5 |
| Fitting | Darnton describes fitting a bass bar with chalk transfer while supporting the top securely; precision emerges from small, repeated adjustments. | R7 |

## Visual direction derived from the research

The following are design decisions, not additional scientific claims.

- **Presence:** Treat the instrument as one uninterrupted object. Use a large crop, a dark field and localized amber light. Narrow highlights should describe varnish thickness and surface curvature without turning the body into polished metal.
- **Material distinction:** Keep spruce grain lengthwise. Let maple figure change with illumination. Give ebony a nearly black, warm surface with restrained grain. The unvarnished bridge should remain lighter than the varnished body.
- **Craft:** Reveal a few related layers with modest separation and retained alignment. Small tool traces, edge shadows and fitted contact surfaces communicate making better than a mechanical explosion.
- **Inside:** Take conceptual inspiration from Charles Brooks's instrument-interior photography: a low viewpoint and convincing wood detail can make a tiny cavity feel monumental. Keep the actual violin structure legible. A fabricated cathedral is not anatomical evidence.
- **First bow:** The hair crosses the strings in the playable span between the bridge and the end of the fingerboard, near the bridge. Hair makes contact; the wooden bow stick does not strike the instrument. Show approach, contact, lateral draw and recurring release. Do not depict a pluck, electric spark or impact detonation.
- **Resonance:** Begin at the contact region and let motion spread through a coherent structure. In a Chladni-like interpretation, calm nodal contours and moving areas should remain distinct. Avoid equalizer bars or an unrelated particle background.
- **Return:** Restore the same recognizable object, crop family and quiet light used at the opening. The journey changes the viewer's understanding of the object.

The two shared elements are **STRING**, a physical line that becomes a wave and returns, and **CURVE**, a common language for body contours, f-hole masks, camera paths and acoustic geometry.

## Asset and interpretation provenance

1. **Research references are not shipped imagery.** A URL in this dossier records the source of knowledge or visual study; it does not imply that the associated photograph is included or licensed for redistribution.
2. **Generated images are synthetic illustrations.** Record the generator, prompts, generation date, chosen files and production transformations in the asset provenance. Do not describe them as photographs of a Stradivari, scans, or measured reconstructions.
3. **Original geometry and effects are illustrative.** Structural relationships should follow the references, while any omitted parts, displaced layers and enlarged motion serve legibility. Avoid unsupported dimensions or assertions of engineering accuracy.
4. **Acoustic graphics are interpretations.** Their visible amplitude and timing are deliberately exaggerated. Decorative contour fields are not measured mode data and should not be assigned experimental mode numbers or exact frequency claims.
5. **Procedural audio is original sound design.** If used, identify it as synthesis rather than a recording of the pictured instrument. Sound should require an explicit user action and should be optional throughout the experience.
6. **Met images require asset-level records if included later.** Record the specific image URL, object metadata, CC0 status and retrieval date. Permission for an object image must not be extended to a museum recording by assumption.

## Visual accuracy review

- Four physical strings; avoid duplicate floating lines that appear to be extra strings.
- A pale bridge with two feet and credible contact on the top.
- A dark fingerboard raised above the body; bow hair contacts the speaking string span.
- Sound post near the treble/E side; bass bar attached below the top on the bass/G side.
- Thin ribs, curved plates and a connected cavity rather than a solid carved block.
- Grain directions and finish identities remain consistent across scene handoffs.
- Contact-driven onset followed by sustained motion; no vibration before contact unless clearly a visual anticipation.
- Interpretive acoustic fields retain the body's curve language and never claim to be measured evidence.
