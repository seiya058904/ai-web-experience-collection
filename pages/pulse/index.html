01The Living Form

Not a symbol. An organ.

Behind the familiar rhythm is a living structure with walls, vessels and a very specific shape.

01

A muscle with an architecture

The heart sits between the lungs, with much of its mass left of the body's midline. Its irregular outline belongs to a muscular pump: receiving chambers above, pumping chambers below, and great vessels at the crown.

02

A surface with an inside

The myocardium forms the working muscle. A cutaway lets us look past the outer contour into the chambers while keeping the organ's orientation fixed. The shape is an illustration; the chamber relationships are the guide.

01

Receive above. Send below.

The right atrium receives the body's returning blood; the left atrium receives blood from the lungs. Each leads to the ventricle below it. The right ventricle serves the lungs, while the left serves the body.

02

A dividing wall

The atrial and ventricular septa keep the right and left sides separate in a healthy heart. The ventricular walls are muscular; the left ventricular wall is thicker than the right. The section makes these relationships visible without reversing them.

Before the push, a signal.

01

Begin in the right atrium

A normal electrical impulse begins in the sinoatrial node, in the right atrium. Activation spreads through the atria and helps coordinate their contraction. These marks describe electrical activity in tissue, not a stream of blood.

02

A pause before the ventricles

Conduction slows at the atrioventricular node, allowing time for ventricular filling. The signal then travels through the ventricular conduction system so the ventricles can contract. This sequence and the circulation route have different meanings and timescales.

Gold dotted marks show electrical activation. Arrowed routes show circulation. The timing is explanatory, not an ECG.

01

Two major entrances

The superior vena cava returns blood from the upper body. The inferior vena cava returns blood from below. Both enter the right atrium. After supplying tissues, this returning blood contains less oxygen; it is still red.

02

Through the tricuspid valve

Blood passes from the right atrium through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle. The valve sits at this chamber boundary. Its position matters: it is neither a passage to the left heart nor the outlet to the lungs.

05One-Way Gates

Doors that answer to pressure.

Four valves open and close with changing pressure, keeping the circulation moving forward.

Compare the valve families

Switch between the chamber entrances and ventricular exits.

Tricuspid: right atrium → right ventricle. Mitral: left atrium → left ventricle. Chordae support the leaflets.

Read the diagram
Atrioventricular valves
Tricuspid: right atrium → right ventricle. Mitral: left atrium → left ventricle. Chordae support the leaflets.
Outflow valves
Pulmonary: right ventricle → pulmonary trunk. Aortic: left ventricle → aorta. Three pocketlike cusps guard each normal outlet.
01

Between receiving and pumping

The tricuspid valve lies between the right atrium and ventricle; the mitral lies between the left pair. Their leaflets are supported by chordae attached to papillary muscles. These supports help keep the leaflets from turning back into the atria.

02

At the two exits

The pulmonary valve guards the right ventricular outlet; the aortic valve guards the left. Their pocketlike semilunar cusps close against returning blood. The inlet and outlet groups work at different phases; the four valves are not all open together.

Comparative valve plates show structure, not simultaneous opening. Outflow valves have no chordae.

07Exchange

Where air meets blood—across a barrier.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide move between alveolar air and the blood through thin tissue layers.

Authored material study · not microscopy

01

Close, but separate

An alveolus contains air. The adjacent capillary contains blood. Their thin tissue barrier allows gases to diffuse between these compartments. Red blood cells remain inside the capillary; the alveolar space is not a blood vessel.

02

Two opposing directions

Oxygen moves from alveolar air into the blood, where hemoglobin carries it. Carbon dioxide moves from blood toward the air to be breathed out. The arrows show these net directions, not visible particles or a measured rate of exchange.

Air and blood remain separate. Only the gases cross the tissue barrier.

From the lungs, pulmonary veins carry oxygen-rich blood into the left side of the heart.

01

A vein can carry oxygen-rich blood

After exchange in the lung capillaries, blood collects into pulmonary veins and returns to the left atrium. A vein is named for its direction toward the heart, not for how much oxygen its blood contains.

02

The left-sided entrance

Blood moves from the left atrium through the mitral valve into the left ventricle. The two returns have different destinations: systemic veins enter the right atrium, pulmonary veins the left. The septum keeps these sides distinct.

09The Stronger Wall

The wall built for distance.

The left ventricle has a thick muscular wall to move blood into the systemic circulation.

Authored material study · not a patient scan

01

Different work, different walls

The normal left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall than the right. It develops the pressure needed for the systemic circulation. The right ventricle serves the pulmonary circulation. This comparison is qualitative, not a measurement of any person's heart.

02

Enter through one valve. Leave through another.

The mitral valve is the inlet from the left atrium. The aortic valve is a distinct outlet into the aorta. During ventricular ejection, the mitral valve closes against backflow as blood leaves through the aortic outlet.

The muscle surrounds the chamber. Inflow and outflow remain distinct.

10Around the Body

One beat, many destinations.

From the aorta, branching arteries reach tissues; blood eventually returns through veins.

The systemic circuit

Compare outward distribution with the return to the heart.

Arteries and veins connect through tissue capillaries. There is no direct aorta-to-vein shortcut in this normal circuit.

Read the diagram
Arterial path
Left ventricle → aorta → systemic arteries → tissue capillaries.
Venous path
Tissue capillaries → systemic veins → venae cavae → right atrium.
Show both
Arteries and veins connect through tissue capillaries. There is no direct aorta-to-vein shortcut in this normal circuit.
01

A branching distribution

The aorta receives blood from the left ventricle. Arteries and smaller branches distribute it to the body's tissues. Capillaries provide the exchange interface; the map condenses many branches into a readable route.

02

A return without a shortcut

Blood leaves tissue capillaries through small veins that join larger venous routes back to the right atrium. This completes the conceptual systemic circuit. Following the route here does not mean a blood cell crosses the whole body in one beat.

11Beat Again

The story has no final beat.

Two connected circuits and a repeating electrical pattern keep a living system moving.

Explore the chapters again
01

Two circuits, one continuous route

The pulmonary circuit connects the right ventricle to the lungs and back to the left atrium. The systemic circuit connects the left ventricle to the body and back to the right atrium. The chambers and valves join these routes in order.

02

Structure carries. The signal coordinates.

Return to the same organ and choose either layer. The blood route describes where transport occurs; the electrical sequence describes how contraction is coordinated. Together they explain a heartbeat without reducing the circulation to a single moment.

The route explains where blood travels. It does not show a complete circulation in one heartbeat.
